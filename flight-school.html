Choosing a Flight School

Where you train has a bigger effect on your cost and timeline than almost anything else. A good school with available airplanes and a consistent instructor can save you months. Here's what to look for.

Part 61 vs. Part 141

Flight schools in the U.S. train under one of two sets of FAA rules:

  • Part 61 is more flexible. Lessons can be scheduled around your life and the training can be adjusted to how you learn. Most independent instructors and smaller schools train this way. The minimum is 40 hours.
  • Part 141 follows an FAA-approved, structured syllabus with stage checks along the way. It's common at larger academies and universities, and the minimum is 35 hours. It's a good fit if you can train full time or want to use certain financial aid or VA benefits.

Neither one is better for everyone. Part 61 usually fits people working or going to school, while Part 141 fits people who want a set structure and can commit to a fast pace.

Where to look

  • Local airports, which almost always have at least one school or independent instructor.
  • Flying clubs, which can be cheaper since members share the cost of the airplanes.
  • College aviation programs if you want a degree alongside your ratings.

Questions to ask before committing

  1. How many airplanes do you have and how hard is it to book them?
  2. How many instructors do you have, and will I fly with the same one each time?
  3. What are the hourly rates for the airplane and the instructor, and are there any extra fees?
  4. What is a realistic average number of hours your students finish in?
  5. Do I have to prepay, and what happens to that money if I leave?

Always take a discovery flight first. How the instructor explains things and how organized the school feels will tell you a lot more than a website will.
