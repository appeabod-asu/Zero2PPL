Licensing Pathway: From Zero to PPL

Getting a Private Pilot License (PPL) can look like a maze from the outside, but it really comes down to a handful of steps that happen in roughly the same order for everyone. Here's how it breaks down, based on the FAA requirements and what I went through myself.

The short version

A 15-second overview of the main milestones on the way to a PPL.

The steps

  1. Take a discovery flight. Most flight schools offer an intro flight where you actually fly the plane with an instructor. It's the cheapest way to find out if you like it before committing.
  2. Get an FAA medical certificate. You'll need at least a third-class medical from an FAA Aviation Medical Examiner (AME) before you can solo. Do this early, because if something comes up it's better to know before you've spent thousands on lessons.
  3. Apply for your student pilot certificate. This is done through the FAA's IACRA system with help from your instructor. You have to be at least 16 to solo, and 17 to get the actual PPL.
  4. Start flying and ground school. Your early lessons cover the basics: takeoffs, landings, turns, stalls, and emergency procedures. Ground school (in person or online) runs alongside and covers weather, airspace, regulations, and navigation.
  5. Solo. Once your instructor signs you off, you'll fly the plane by yourself for the first time, usually a few laps around the traffic pattern. It's the moment most pilots remember forever.
  6. Cross-country and night training. You'll plan and fly longer trips to other airports, both with your instructor and solo, plus some night flying.
  7. Pass the knowledge test. A multiple choice written exam taken at a testing center. You need a 70% to pass.
  8. Pass the checkride. The final exam with an FAA Designated Pilot Examiner (DPE). It has an oral portion on the ground and a flight portion where you show you can safely fly to the Airman Certification Standards.

Want the official wording? The full requirements are listed in the FAA regulations at 14 CFR Part 61, Subpart E, and AOPA's Learn to Fly page is a good plain-English starting point.
